    • First lets set some groundwork to talk about how our brains work.

    • In 'Thinking Fast and Slow' they talk about two mechanisms in the mind to help discuss different phenomenon of cognition.

      • System 1:
        • Constantly running
        • Automatic
        • Almost instantaneous
        • Responsible for Intuition
        • Little or no voluntary control
        • Can be done in the bg, e.g. walking and talking
      • System 2:
        • Effortful, when we have to stop and think
        • Much slower
        • Responsible for conscious choice and concentration
        • If I ask you what is 17x24, the vast majority of us will have to stop and think
        • If we were walking you might even pause walking to think because System 2 can take that much effort

    • If System 1 realizes it has a problem, it will pull in System 2

    • These two systems help us make it through the day,
      • it isn't possible to use System 2 for all of our decision making
    • While this way of functioning has a lot of advantages,
      • it also has downsides that we call 'Cognitive errors'

  • A quick thought exercise to demonstrate these two concepts in your own mind:

      • Asking this question, I could have made it much easier for your intuitive sense and said, 'the bat costs $1.05, how much does the ball cost?', the intuitive error occurs because of the comparison.
    • If you caught on to my trick,
    • or System 1 pulled in System 2,
      • you'd answer slower and are more likely to come up with the right answer.

'Dunning Kruger Effect and Overconfidence'

  • If you're not familiar, 'The Dunning Kruger' came around in 1999
    • It describes a common phenomenon (with all of us) that when we are new at a skill, our perceived skill is often much higher than our actual skill.
    • This study is not about specific types of people, it describes a tendency that we've all exhibited and will likely do so again.
  • In a book I read recently called 'Think Again' by Adam Grant
  • He talks about study conducted of new doctors,
    • These people are not unintelligent, and certainly have plenty of knowledge
    • but they do lack experience with patients
      • (Graph Carmen Sanchez & David Dunning 'Overconfidence Among Beginners: Is a Little Learning a Dangerous Thing', 'Think Again - Stranded at the Summit of Mount Stupid)
    • In this 2018 study, they mapped that confidence of the doctor's diagnosis vs if it turned out to be true.
    • As they gained a little experience, their confidence outpaced their actual accuracy very quickly, and at least in the study, it continued to outpace accuracy.

Substituting Questions:

  • Our minds will subconsciously substitute easier questions instead of trying to figure out the actual (harder) question,
    • For instance: (Kahneman, 2011; Kahneman & Frederick, 2002)
    • For example:
      • If asked to donate to a non-profit, the question you're probably answering is 'how many stories of this problem can I think of' and 'how strongly do I feel about it'
      • If asked how safe it is in a city or country, you're probably going to answer the question 'how many stories about crime or violence can I think of' and maybe questioning how much you've even heard about the place.
    • Intuitive judgments from System 1 rely on these substitutions

'What you see is all there is'

  • Daniel Kahneman in 'Thinking Fast and Slow' coined 'What you see is all there is'
  • System 1 doesn't tend to consider if you are missing information
  • It uses the information we have as if it is the only information, and most of the time we are unaware this even happening

  • Giving and Receiving Criticism

    • In art school, almost every class we'd set our pieces up on the wall and critique other people's work. We learned very quickly how to give and receive feedback.
    • Giving:
      • Prefer:
        • Start with overall feeling, especially if it's positive
        • Ask clarifying questions about intent, desired outcome, audience
        • Identify root problems/issues that you're sensing without going to solutions
        • Use language like 'I think', 'I'm seeing' to allow room for other opinions, esp in a group setting
      • Avoid:
        • Expressing thoughts or opinions as fact
        • Giving solutions to anything but the simplest issues, focus on why the solution came to mind, what were you solving for
        • Focusing on any one point for long
    • Receiving
      • Prefer:
        • Acknowledge feedback
        • Ask clarifying questions, make sure you understand their lens
        • Make sure you understand how strongly they feel about the piece of feedback, is it strongly felt, a quibble, a thought?
        • Thank them for their feedback, good critique is effortful and can be stressful
        • Ask if specific follow up is expected
      • Avoid:
        • Respond to criticism in the moment, unless there's a strong disconnect
        • Taking it personally, it should be about the work

    • Code review

      • It should always feel like a conversation, not an edict
        • If you are responsible for the outcome and empowered (lead of an effort/team, manager, etc) and need a change make sure to explain why. What is the problem, risk, or outcome you are avoiding.
      • Provide context for how important each item is, is it critical, preferred, or ideal but not necessary.
      • Add a note if you really like something or learned something new.

  • Losing heat through your head

    • The myth of losing most of your heat through your head stems from an Arctic survival experiment where everything but their head was covered. They estimated upwards of 40% of your body heat was lost through your head in those conditions.
    • The factoid of 'we lose 40% of our body heat through our heads' was picked up and printed in a 1970 Army manual, become a popular myth.
    • If the experimenters had rerun the experiment with different parts of the body uncovered, they would have found the correct answer, we lose about the same amount of heat from any part of our body.
    • But the publishers saw the factoid, and that it came from a study and reprinted it without questioning the assertion out of context.

      • We're not making an bad work environment

          • *unless it's pertinent to the job
            • I've had to have conversations about sexual misconduct happening at work, unfair hiring practices, etc
        • Maintain respectful communication
          • I find if I'm struggling on this, something needs to happen. A discussion with the person about issues, mediation, discussions with managers or HR, or removing myself from the situation.
          • No yelling, or outbursts of anger
            • If you've reached that point, it is not the time to fix it, it's the time to deescalate and start figuring out how to address it

  • Have the tough conversations

    • Since we are imperfect, we know we need to have tough conversations to have better results.
    • I've learned a lot from Non-Violent Communication, Active Listening, and going to Counseling, highly recommend looking into all of these things.
    • When the tough conversation is yours:
      • Is it Kind, Helpful, and Informative?
        • I've heard many versions of this, I like this one because it doesn't put a burden on the message to be positive, and we need to be able to talk about things that aren't going well.
        • Kind over nice. Kind means talking with empathy and respect, nice can mean diluting the message, being protective of the audience, and can be condescending.
        • Helpful, can bringing it up benefit us? Will it improve something, increase understanding, will this be appreciated, or is it needed (even if unfortunate and hard to appreciate)
        • Informative, avoid explaining what is known, or adding data that isn't applicable.
          • If it falls on deaf ears, it is not informative
      • Avoid 'you' language, heavily favor 'I' language
      • When describing something, acknowledge it's what 'you're seeing', avoid thinking or saying things as facts
        • Ask questions instead of sharing judgments
      • Try to describe what happened without judgmental words
      • Consider the setting and power dynamic, especially if you might have more
        • Will the setting or relationship:
          • make embarrassment more likely?
          • will vagueness cause worry
      • State vs Trait - It's something that's happening, not something baked in
    • When the tough conversation is brought to you:
      • Don't skip to answers; out loud, or in your head. Stay in investigator mode
      • Make sure it's a safe setting for them, if it isn't, make one
      • Help the others feel heard
        • Ask questions about what they mean, what they think might happen, how they feel
        • Reflect the feeling you think they might be experiencing (that sounds awful, or that sounds great) (Active Listening)
        • Ask if they're looking for help, or if they needed to vent
      • Encourage talking and thinking about the issues in a helpful way
        • Help avoid judgmental language, as it is generally not helpful
        • Encourage thinking of issues as a state, not a trait

    • If have formal power (lead/manager/etc)
      • Making sure you understand your surroundings, sponge period
      • Delegate well, share the challenges and successes
      • Communicate your strategy, how it plays into your approach, and what you expect to see happen
      • Communicate what you're confidence level, allow others the room to ask questions and collaborate
    • If you do not have formal power (consultant, smaller role)
      • Sponge Period!
      • Make sure you understand the goals and how they'll be measured
      • Show that your willing to help
      • If the leads are overwhelmed, identify areas they could delegate to you
      • Don't be afraid to ask questions, especially if something doesn't make sense
      • Encourage working together

  • The Downside of all of this

    • None of this comes easily, it requires thought and effort.
    • Change is usually difficult
    • Diversity of thought, being able to challenge your beliefs tilts the odds in your favor, but it is not the easier path.

'Confirmation Bias'

  • We routinely overemphasize data that agrees with our beliefs,
  • Or create a narrative to discount data that doesn't, spoiler alert
  • There is a strong correlation with intelligence and difficulty not doing this

'The Halo Effect'

  • With incomplete System 1 will fill in facts based on it's overall impression. For example, substituting the person's attractiveness for their trustworthiness or competence.
