Cursed
Feedback Loops

  • Sometimes we have cursed feedback loops,
  • when a feedback loop incentivizes the wrong thing
  • Daniel Kahneman is a Nobel Prize winning psychologist
    he was teaching Air Force flight instructors
    the psychology of effective training
  • Kahneman was giving a speech about
    an important principle of skill training:
  • rewards for improved performance work better than punishment for mistakes.
  • After his 'enthusiastic speech', one of the
    seasoned pilot instructors stood up to say
    positive reinforcement wasn't optimal for cadets
  • In his long experience when
    he praised a cadet for a good maneuver,
    the next time they performed worse!
  • On the other hand,
    when he screams into their headset
    after a bad maneuver the often improved the next time.
  • The instructor was experience is accurate,
    but also completely wrong.

Regression to the Mean

From: Daniel Kahneman. Thinking Fast and Slow "Regression to the Mean"

  • So on a large time scale, professional athletes, software devs, cooks, all skilled humans have 'good days' and 'bad days'

Regression to the Mean

  • Our performance can be thought of as a sin curve,
    • We have an average performance, and fluxuations around that
    • Michael Jordan's sin curve for three pointers is on a whole other level than mine, but we both have a sin curve.
  • When you perform well, it's most likely that your next performance is average,
    • which may look like you've done poorer
  • When you perform poorly, it's most likely that your next performance is average,
    • which will look like an improvement
  • So the flight instructor's experience is described accurately.
  • Daniel Kahneman is saying, is that his praise or beratement isn't what caused the performance to change.
    • From long term research and science, we know that positive reinforcement is much more effective than negative reinforcement
  • A quick note, this isn't to say negative reinforcement doesn't happen, but that it should be avoided.

Intuition, Bias, and Confidence

  • This is a big topic, we'll be doing a bit of a speed run
  • First, I need to show you a way of conceptualizing how cognition works.
  • This is also from Daniel Kahneman's Thinking Fast and Slow
  • In the book, he describes two mechanisms simply called "System 1" & "System 2"
  • These aren't parts of the brain's anatomy, but a way to describe how cognition works

System 1

  • Constantly running
  • Automatic and almost instantaneous responses
  • Responsible for intuition, our "gut"
  • Little or no voluntary control
  • Tasks system 1 controls often feel effortless
  • Walking and talking is a good example, we have lots of experience and doing both isn't a problem

System 2

  • Effortful and much slower than System 1
  • Only runs when needed
  • Responsible for conscious choice and concentration
  • The compliment to system 1
  • If we're walking and talking,
    and I asked you to quickly figure out 17 x 24
  • You'll have to think for a second, you might even stop walking
  • All day we have to make a myriad of little decisions
  • The vast majority are solved by system 1
  • It isn't possible to use System 2 all day
  • A nice feature, if System 1 realizes it has a problem, it will pull in System 2

System 1 absorbs:

  • Your areas expertise
  • The information your exposed to
  • If you're a chess expert, eventually System 1 can look at a chess game and figure out checkmate
  • As a CSS expert, I have a high success rate of finding layout bugs quickly and being able to fix them
  • This determines a large amount of your 'lens', how you see the world
    It is largely responsible for your unconscious bias
  • While this way of functioning has a lot of advantages,
    • it also has downsides, what we call 'cognitive errors'

Cognitive Errors

  • Before I get too much into them, I've got bad news.
    • Knowing these about cognitive errors, doesn't mean you're less likely to have them.
    • These are bugs in the machine, we all experience them, all the time.
    • Good news is with awareness of them we can compensate
    • in important situations, maybe it's high risk or reward;
    • we can make sure we stop to make sure we aren't falling into a mistake because of cognitive ease.
  • Now I want to ask you to consider a word problem, and pay attention to what's in your mind.
  • A bat and a ball
    together cost $1.10
  • The bat costs
    $1 more than the ball.
  • How much does the ball cost?
  • A bat and a ball
    together cost $1.10
  • Intuitive Answer: 10¢
  • Then the bat would be $1.10
    the total would be $1.20
  • In studies the of us get this wrong,
    I'd bet this answer occurred most of us, even if it wasn't what we decided
  • This is what System 1 might have presented, it 'feels right'
  • A bat and a ball
    together cost $1.10
  • Correct Answer: 5¢.
  • Then the bat would be $1.05
    the total would be $1.10
  • You may have realized I was playing a trick
  • Or double checked your result, this would mean System 2 took over
  • The trick in this word problem, is giving a sense of cognitive ease, with nice round numbers, and the price comparison of the bat, which sounds simple

    • Dunning Kruger & Overconfidence

    • You may have heard of it, there's a common misconception though.
    • It describes a phenomenon that when we to a skill, our perceived skill is often much higher than our actual skill.
    • It is not limited to people that aren't smart/cool enough. We all are likely to exhibit it in the future, and have certainly done it before now.
    • In a book I read recently called 'Think Again' by Adam Grant
    • He talks about a 2018 study conducted of new residents,
      • These people are not unintelligent, and certainly have plenty of knowledge out of med school.
      • but they do lack experience diagnosing patients
    Carmen Sanchez & David Dunning 'Overconfidence Among Beginners: Is a Little Learning a Dangerous Thing',
    'Think Again - Stranded at the Summit of Mount Stupid)
    • In this chart, the blue line is showing how accurate the residents feel their diagnoses are
      with a gray line showing how accurate it ended up being
    • Before they've seen any patients, their confidence is lower than their ability
    • But before they've even seen 10, their confidence outpaces their ability
    • And at least in the study, the lines never met
    • Let me give a personal example,
      • I've considered myself an HTML expert for a long time
      • For many years, I felt I was also adept at web accessibility
      • After meeting many actual experts, I can tell you I was very mistaken about my skill level
      • I didn't know:
        • the spec isn't always right,
        • that assistive tech lags behind and has weird implementations,
        • and to be an expert I'll need to learn to use some assistive tech to understand the experience

    Substituting Questions

    • When asked a difficult question, System 1 will subconsciously substitute easier questions instead of trying to figure out the hard one.
    • For example:
      • If asked to donate to a non-profit for a good cause, the question you're probably answering is
        • how many stories of this problem can I think of
        • how strongly do I feel about it'
        • does someone/thing I care about threatened by this issue
      • At work, I often run into this when estimating a task.
        • Earlier in my career, system 1 was causing a lot of mistakes in my estimate,
        • I wasn't answering the right questions.
        • Now I have better questions, and know I when it's important, I need to stop and think about my estimate
    • Which leads me into my next cognitive error, that has a good mouth feel

    Encourage (or Find) a
    Learning Culture

    • I add find because if you aren't getting it from work,
      finding one is the next best thing, and in my experience, can help you to a better job.

    Learning Culture thrives on two qualities:

    • Psychological Safety
    • Accountability

    Psychological Safety

    Is about respect, kindness, & trust
    • If I mess up or report an issue, talking about it won't have a negative result.
    • I can question the direction or decisions of our leadership and I won't be punished.
    • I can have hard conversations when they're needed.
    • As Psychological Safety has gotten popular in the last many years, I've heard it misconstrued a lot.
    • I don't think it's always intentional, so I'd like to be specific about what it is and is not.

    Psychological Safety

    • Is not:
      • Being "nice" and agreeable
      • Seeing mistakes as threats
      • Keeping ideas to yourself
      • Only touting your strengths
      • Fear of teammates and supervisors
    • Kind over nice
      • Kind takes other's feelings into account, and is honest
      • Nice is protective, it may omit the negative, and inflate the positive
      If you can't talk about what's wrong, it's a big problem.

    Accountability

    Without it, there is no reason to bring up issues.
    The goal should not be guilt, shame, or punishment; but us agains the problem.
    • You can have psychological safety without accountability, but it means we're likely to stay in our comfort zone.
    • If change can't be effected, why expend the effort.
    • Not all feedback can be addressed, but change from feedback should not be uncommon
    • When I've been in a leadership position and I get feedback, at a minimum I want to make sure they feel heard.

    Tying it up

    • Too often we tie our identity or success to opinions, ideas, tools;
    • But all of these can change quite often
    • Ideas and tools are fashion, constantly evolving and changing; and changing our identity takes a lot of work.
    • Spend some time and think about what your values are, what makes good work and a good workplace for you?
    • Organize your identity on those values, and you'll be in a better frame of mind to meet your goals.
